Before you start

Two self-tests and the red flags

Red flags

See a clinician rather than working through this page:

  • weakness that persists when the shoulder isn't painful;
  • the arm feeling like it might drop;
  • night pain that reliably wakes you, especially lying on that side;
  • a clear moment when something changed and the arm stopped working properly.

Where does it actually hurt?

Press firmly into the outside of the upper arm, roughly a third of the way down from the point of the shoulder toward the elbow — not at the elbow, and not on the shoulder tip itself. That is the deltoid tuberosity, where the deltoid attaches to the humerus; it is a broad roughened ridge rather than a distinct lump, so hunt around a little. True deltoid insertional tendinopathy is sharply tender to that pressure. Referred rotator-cuff pain usually isn't — the area aches vaguely but pressing on it doesn't reproduce the pain. This matters because the deltoid insertion is a classic referral zone; the tissue that hurts and the tissue driving it are often several inches apart.

Is the neck involved?

Extend the neck, side-bend toward the painful side, hold a few seconds. If that reproduces the arm pain, the cervical spine is contributing, and the neck work belongs in your program.

During a flare-up

For days when the shoulder is too sore for normal training. Doing this beats resting completely — resting makes tendon pain worse over a few weeks.

Isometric external rotation

What to do when the shoulder is too sore for the rest of the program.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Stand side-on to a doorframe with the sore arm nearest it.
  2. Bend that elbow to 90° and tuck it against your ribs, palm facing in, back of the wrist touching the frame.
  3. Press the back of the wrist outward into the frame at about 70% effort. Nothing moves.
  4. Hold for the count, relax fully, repeat.
Effort
About 70% of maximum
Hold
30–45 seconds × 4–5 reps
Frequency
2–3 times a day

Watch forThe elbow drifting away from your side, or shrugging. Nothing should move.

Why it's here

Isometric holds — push hard, nothing moves — produce short-term pain relief in tendinopathy (an overloaded tendon that has become irritable) without the tissue stress of moving through range, which means you can keep loading the cuff rather than resting it — and rest reliably makes tendinopathy worse over the medium term. The evidence for the analgesic effect is mixed across studies, but it is low-risk and costs nothing to try.

Source: Prehab Guys · Rehab Hero

Rear cuff & shoulder blade

Climbing and paddling pull the arm inward and forward. These train the muscles that pull it back out, and the ones that position the shoulder blade.

Side-lying external rotation

Trains the muscles that rotate the arm outward — the opposite of every climbing pull.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Lie on your side with the sore arm on top.
  2. Bend that elbow to 90° and pin it against your ribs, forearm resting across your belly, light weight in the hand.
  3. Draw the shoulder blade back first, then rotate the forearm up toward the ceiling.
  4. Lower slowly. The elbow stays at 90° and glued to your side throughout.
Start
3 × 10–15 light
Progress to
3–4 × 6–10
Tempo
3 seconds up, 3 seconds down
Frequency
2–3 sessions a week
Note
Heavy slow resistance. The tempo matters more than the rep count.

Watch forStraightening the elbow as you rotate. Keep it at 90° throughout.

Why it's here

Climbing and surf paddling are overwhelmingly internal-rotation dominant. This loads infraspinatus and teres minor — the main external rotators — in a position where the shoulder blade can't do the work instead. Starting the motion with the shoulder blade pulling back, then the arm, is what separates this from a wrist exercise.

Source: Prehab Guys

Banded external rotation, arm out at 45°

The same outward rotation, trained with the arm raised, closer to where it hurts.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Anchor a light band at about elbow height and stand side-on, holding it in the hand of the sore arm so it pulls across your body.
  2. Lift that arm out from your side to about 45° — halfway between hanging down and straight out — elbow bent to 90°.
  3. Holding that arm position, rotate the forearm back and up against the band.
  4. Return slowly. Only the forearm moves; the upper arm stays put.
Sets & reps
3 × 10–12 per side
Frequency
2–3 times a week
Load
Light band
Note
These muscles are small and fatigue fast.

Watch forThe torso twisting to help. Keep the body still.

Why it's here

Working the cuff only with the elbow at your side builds strength in a position climbing never uses. Moving the arm out to 45° starts loading the cuff closer to the abducted, rotated positions where symptoms show up. Add it once the 0° version is comfortable.

Source: Aurora Prime Physiotherapy · Wellen · OrthoInfo

Wall slide with lift-off

Trains the shoulder blade to rotate properly as the arm goes overhead.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Face a wall about a forearm's length back. Put both forearms on it, elbows at shoulder height, little-finger edge of each hand against the wall.
  2. Press into the wall and slide the forearms up, letting the shoulder blades travel up and around.
  3. At the top, lift both forearms an inch off the wall and hold.
  4. Put them back and slide down. Keep the ribs down — don't arch your back to get higher.
Sets & reps
2–3 × 8–10 slides
Hold
3–5 seconds each lift-off
Frequency
Low enough load to do daily.

Watch forThe lower back arching to make up range the shoulder doesn't have.

Why it's here

Trains upward rotation of the shoulder blade — the movement that clears space for the rotator cuff as the arm rises. The lift-off at the top is the part that recruits lower trapezius; without it this is mostly a mobility drill. Pressing into the wall through the pinky side of the hands and the elbows throughout is what keeps serratus engaged.

Source: Prehab Guys — wall slide · lift-off variant

Prone Y raise

Trains the lower trapezius, which pulls the shoulder blade down and back.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Lie face down with your forehead on a folded towel.
  2. Reach both arms overhead along the floor, angled about 45° out from your body, thumbs pointing up — a Y shape.
  3. Slide the shoulder blades down toward your back pockets, then lift the arms a few inches.
  4. Hold at the top, then lower slowly.
Sets & reps
2–3 × 10–15
Hold
2–3 seconds at the top
Note
Lower trap is endurance-biased, so higher reps at light load are right here.

Watch forShrugging. If you feel it in the upper trapezius, at the top of the shoulder, lift less high.

Why it's here

Standing up, gravity pulls in roughly the same direction the lower trapezius does, so the muscle barely has to work. Lying face down turns gravity into resistance: the lower trapezius has to do the lifting, and stronger muscles cannot substitute for it. This is standard in shoulder rehab for exactly that reason.

Source: Ethos PT · Prehab Guys — prone Y · prone lift-off

Face pull

High-rep work for the back of the shoulder. Hard to do badly.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Set a band or cable at about head height. Hold one end in each hand, palms facing each other.
  2. Step back until there is tension, arms straight out in front of you.
  3. Pull toward your forehead, driving the elbows out wide and high.
  4. At the end, rotate your hands back so the knuckles point behind you. Return slowly.
Sets & reps
3 × 12–20
Load
Light to moderate

Watch forTurning it into a row. The external rotation at the end is the point.

Why it's here

Combines pulling the arms out to the sides with rotating them back — close to the exact opposite of the climbing pull pattern. You can do a lot of it without much risk, so it's an easy place to add reps.

Source: Citizen Athletics · Rehab Hero

Shoulder blade control

Serratus anterior is the muscle that holds the shoulder blade flat against the ribs. If it is weak, the blade lifts away and the rotator cuff has nothing steady to pull against.

Push-up plus

Strengthens the muscle that holds the shoulder blade flat against the ribs.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Get into a push-up position — on the floor, or hands on a bench or wall if the floor provokes it.
  2. Lower into the push-up and press back up as normal.
  3. At the top, with the elbows already straight, keep pushing so the shoulder blades spread apart and your upper back rounds slightly.
  4. Release and repeat. That last push is the exercise.
Sets & reps
3 × 10–15
Frequency
2–3 times a week
Easier version
Wall or incline version if the floor version provokes symptoms.

Watch forStopping at the top of the push-up. That last push, spreading the shoulder blades apart, is the exercise.

Why it's here

Serratus anterior holds the shoulder blade flat against the ribcage. When it is weak the blade lifts away from the ribs — this is called winging — and the rotator cuff no longer has a steady base to pull against. A push-up is closed-chain — your hands stay planted — so the blade can move freely on the ribcage while bodyweight supplies the resistance. The "plus" at the top is the protraction, pushing the shoulder blades apart, and that is the part that does the work.

Source: Musculoskeletal Physiotherapy · Rehab Hero · Prehab Guys

Bear-position scapular push-up

The same muscle as the push-up plus, slower and harder to cheat.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Start on hands and knees — hands under the shoulders, knees under the hips.
  2. Tuck your toes and lift the knees an inch or two off the floor. Hold there with a flat back.
  3. Keeping the elbows straight, let the chest sink slightly so the shoulder blades squeeze together.
  4. Push the floor away so the blades spread apart again. Slow, and only the blades move.
Sets & reps
3 × 8–12
Tempo
Slow.

Watch forThe hips rising or the back sagging. The only thing that moves is the shoulder blades.

Why it's here

Knees hovering, spine neutral, moving only the shoulder blades. Works serratus on its own, without loading the shoulder through its range, which makes it usable even on days when pressing is uncomfortable.

Source: Rehab Hero

Kettlebell plank drag-through

Makes the shoulder blade hold still while your body is pulled sideways.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Get into a push-up position with the hands a little wider than the shoulders and the feet wide apart.
  2. Put a kettlebell on the floor just outside one hand.
  3. Reach under your chest with the opposite hand and drag the bell across to the other side.
  4. Put the hand back down, reset, and drag it back. The hips stay level — nothing twists.
Sets & reps
3 × 6–10 drags per side
Stop at
Form degrading, not at a number.

Watch forThe shoulder blade lifting off the ribcage on the supporting arm.

Why it's here

A progression that adds twisting force underneath a shoulder blade that has to stay still — which is what happens in nearly every overhead sport. The published breakdown is unusually good on what failure looks like: the shoulder blade lifts away from the ribs while the movement still looks impressive.

Source: [P]rehab

Cuff under load

Keeping the shoulder joint steady while holding something unstable. This is what is not working when your arm hurts on a steering wheel.

Bottoms-up kettlebell press

Forces the rotator cuff to steady a wobbling weight. Light and slow on purpose.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Hold a light kettlebell by the handle with the bell upside down above your fist.
  2. Grip hard and bring it to shoulder height — forearm vertical, wrist straight, elbow under the bell.
  3. Press straight up until the arm locks out, keeping the bell balanced above you.
  4. Lower under control. If the bell keeps tipping, go lighter.
Sets & reps
3 × 5–8 per arm
Frequency
1–2 times a week
Load
Capped by grip and stability, not strength, so it stays light.

Watch forRushing. The whole value is in the control.

Why it's here

Inverting the bell makes the load unstable, which forces the cuff to brace automatically, without you thinking about it, to keep the humeral head — the ball of the shoulder joint — centred. You can't muscle through it with the deltoid — the bell falls. That reflexive stabilization under sustained low-level load is exactly what a shoulder that hurts on a steering wheel is failing to do.

Source: Mike Reinold

Bottoms-up carry

The same as the press, but you hold the position instead of repeating it.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Hold a light kettlebell upside down at shoulder height, elbow bent, forearm vertical.
  2. Grip hard and brace your trunk so the bell stays balanced.
  3. Walk for the distance, or just stand still for the time, keeping it upright.
  4. Put it down before your grip fails, then swap sides.
Carries
3–4 per arm
Each
20–30 seconds, or 20–40 metres

Watch forThe wrist bending. Keep the forearm stacked under the bell.

Why it's here

Closer to the real demand — holding a position on the wall, or a steering wheel — than any rep-based version.

Source: Chris Liddle · Rehab Hero

Turkish get-up

Moves the shoulder through its full range under weight, and shows which positions you can't control.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Lie on your back holding a light weight straight up over one shoulder. Bend the same-side knee and put that foot flat on the floor.
  2. Roll onto the opposite forearm, then onto that hand, pushing up to sitting. The weight stays locked overhead.
  3. Lift your hips, sweep the straight leg back underneath you into a half-kneeling position, then stand up.
  4. Reverse every step to get back down. Keep your eyes on the weight through the early steps.
Reps
3–5 per side
Load
Very light or unloaded — a shoe balanced on the fist works — for the first several weeks.
Note
This is a skill before it's a strength exercise.

Watch forLosing sight of the weight. Eyes stay on it through the early phases.

Why it's here

Takes the shoulder through full range under continuous load while the torso moves underneath it — which is what actually happens surfing and climbing. The points where it breaks down tend to be the points where control is missing, so it tells you something as well as trains something. Before loading it, check you can get that arm fully overhead against a wall without arching your back.

Source: Cat & Chau · Rehab Hero

Overhead strength

Pressing weight overhead, starting at an angle the shoulder tolerates and getting steeper as it improves.

Overhead pressing progression

Builds overhead pressing strength in three stages, starting at an easier angle.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Landmine press. Wedge one end of a barbell into a corner. Hold the other end at shoulder height in one hand.
  2. Stand square with the ribs down and press the bar up and away along its natural arc, then lower slowly.
  3. Incline press. Same movement on an inclined bench, once the landmine version is comfortable.
  4. Overhead press. Straight up, last. Add weight at each stage before you make the angle steeper.
Sets & reps
3 × 8–12
Strength work
1–2 times a week
Mobility work
3–4 times a week
Note
The supporting mobility work usually goes in the warm-up.

Watch forThe ribs lifting and the lower back arching to find range the shoulder doesn't have.

Why it's here

The landmine angle sits roughly in the scapular plane — the direction the shoulder blade naturally faces — which is mechanically kinder to the tendons running under the bony tip of the shoulder than pressing straight overhead. That lets you load the pressing pattern before full overhead range is comfortable, then progress the angle as tolerance improves.

Source: Simone Sports Performance · Rehab Hero · Prehab Guys

Mobility

Daily, in the warm-up. About ten minutes for all three.

Cross-body adduction stretch

Stretches the back of the shoulder, which tightens up from overhead sport.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Stand, or lie on your back, and bring the sore arm straight across your chest.
  2. Use the other hand to pull it in — grip just above the elbow, not at the wrist.
  3. Keep that shoulder pressed back and down. Don't let it roll forward toward your chin.
  4. Hold and breathe normally. Doing it lying down, or with your back to a wall, makes the shoulder easier to keep back.
Hold
30 seconds × 3–5 per side
Frequency
Daily or most days

Watch forLetting the shoulder roll forward. That's the usual error and it defeats the whole stretch. Keep the ball of the joint back — doing it against a wall or lying down helps.

Why it's here

The muscles and capsule at the back of the shoulder tighten in the dominant arm of overhead athletes, and that tightness limits how far the arm can rotate inward and how far it can come across the chest — which shifts the ball of the shoulder joint forward during overhead motion. Research comparing this to the sleeper stretch found the cross-body approach significantly more effective at restoring internal rotation.

Source: Prehab Guys

Foam roller thoracic extension

Restores mid-back extension, so the shoulder doesn't have to supply it.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Lie on your back with a foam roller across your mid-back, just under the shoulder blades.
  2. Support your head in both hands and keep your hips on the floor.
  3. Let your upper back arch backwards over the roller. Either hold there, or do slow repeated extensions.
  4. Move the roller a few inches up or down and repeat at two or three spots.
Hold
About 2 minutes at each of 2–3 segments
Or
10–15 slow extensions per segment
Frequency
Most days
Note
Use the reps version if sustained holds are uncomfortable.

Watch forThe pelvis lifting. Keep it down so the extension happens in the thoracic spine, not the lumbar.

Why it's here

When the thoracic spine — the mid-back — won't extend, the shoulder joint itself has to produce range it doesn't have, loading the cuff at end range. Nearly every surfer and climber is stiff here.

Source: Rehab My Patient · Rehab Hero

Doorway pec stretch

Stretches the chest muscles that pull the shoulder forward when you sit.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Stand in a doorway and put one forearm flat against the frame, elbow at about shoulder height.
  2. Step the same-side foot through the doorway.
  3. Turn your chest gently away from that arm until you feel a stretch across the front of the chest.
  4. Hold. Ease off if you feel it pinching in the front of the shoulder joint instead.
Hold
30 seconds per side × 2–3
Frequency
Daily

Watch forOver-stretching into the front of the shoulder joint. It should be felt across the chest.

Why it's here

A short pectoralis minor — a small muscle running under the chest to the shoulder blade — tips the shoulder blade forward — the same postural fault that sitting at a steering wheel with unsupported arms produces.

Source: Medbridge · Rehab Hero

Neck

Conditional. Take the neck self-test at the top to find out whether this applies to you.

Chin nod, lying down

Neck strengthening. Only if the neck self-test reproduced the arm pain.
Watch the demo

How to do it

  1. Lie on your back with your knees bent and your head resting flat on the floor.
  2. Without lifting your head, nod your chin down toward your throat — a small 'yes' movement.
  3. Hold, keeping the front of your neck soft and slack.
  4. Release all the way between reps.
Hold
5 seconds
Sets & reps
3 × 10

Watch forThe front of the neck bulging. That's sternocleidomastoid, the rope-like muscle down the side of the neck, doing the work instead of the deep ones.

Why it's here

If the C5 nerve root is contributing to the deltoid-region pain, cuff work alone won't be enough. Building endurance in the deep cervical flexors — the small muscles at the front of the neck — addresses the forward-head posture that loads the upper cervical segments — the same posture driving produces.

Source: Physiohealth01 · Rehab Hero

How often, and for how long

Mobility daily or near-daily, in the warm-up. Rear cuff, shoulder blade and serratus work three times a week. Cuff-under-load and overhead pressing twice a week, ideally not the day after hard climbing.

Expect three to four months before the shoulder is meaningfully better. The most common way this fails is doing it for three weeks, feeling better, and stopping before the weight ever got heavy enough to change anything.

    What this isn't

    This is a personal reference assembled from public physiotherapy sources. It isn't a diagnosis, and nobody has examined this shoulder. Pain at the deltoid insertion has several plausible causes that are distinguished by physical examination — the rotator cuff, the tissues under the bony tip of the shoulder, the neck, or the deltoid insertion itself — and the rehab differs between them. An in-person assessment is worth it mainly for that.

    The rep and set numbers here come from tendon-loading research and ordinary clinical practice. Reasonable clinicians prescribe differently. What actually matters is adding weight steadily over months, staying inside pain you can tolerate.
